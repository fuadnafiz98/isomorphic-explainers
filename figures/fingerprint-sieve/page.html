How browser fingerprinting identifies you

A fingerprint is not stored on your device. It is worked out again on every visit, from how your browser and hardware answer a handful of questions. That is why clearing cookies does not remove it.

__FIGURE__

1 · The idea in plain words

A cookie is a name tag a site hands you. You can take it off. A fingerprint is the site recognising your face: nothing was handed over, and nothing can be taken off.

No single answer is rare. Millions of people run the latest Chrome, millions have the same laptop, millions read French. But the people who have all of those, plus your GPU, your fonts, your screen and your time zone, are a much smaller crowd. Keep adding answers and the crowd shrinks until it is one browser. The figure is that process as a machine: the ball is you, each sieve is one kind of question, and only the part of the crowd that answers the way you do falls through to the next one.

2 · Step by step: what happens when you open a page

  1. The request leaves. It carries your IP address and headers such as User-Agent and Accept-Language. The server learns these before any script runs, which is why the W3C calls this passive fingerprinting.
  2. The page arrives with a script. Often not the site's own: an ad or analytics script embedded on thousands of sites runs the same probes everywhere.
  3. The script probes your device. This is active fingerprinting, and it needs no permission prompt. It draws to a hidden canvas, asks WebGL for the GPU name, renders a tone offline, measures text in candidate fonts and reads the screen, time zone and core count.
  4. The readings become one string. The values are joined in a fixed order. Any single change produces a different string.
  5. The string is hashed. The hash is the ID. The demo at the bottom of this page uses SHA-256; many libraries use faster non-cryptographic hashes.
  6. The server looks the ID up. Seen before? Then you are the same visitor, and whatever else is attached to that ID (an account, an ad profile, a purchase history) comes with you, across every site that embeds the same script.

3 · Why a handful of answers is enough: counting bits

The unit is the bit. A trait shared by one person in n is worth log₂(n) bits, because that is how many times it halves the crowd.

Independent traits add. 33 bits is 2³³, about 8.6 billion, more than the number of people alive, so 33 bits of answers is, in principle, enough to single out anyone. The seven sieves in the figure are worth exactly 33 bits in total; the split between them is illustrative.

The catch is the word independent. A Mac, an M-series chip and Safari tell you almost the same thing, so their bits overlap and the real total is lower than the sum. Researchers therefore measure the bits of the whole fingerprint rather than adding up traits. EFF's 2010 Panopticlick study put a lower bound at 18.1 bits, meaning that at best one browser in 286,777 shared a fingerprint. Its explanatory framing is that a random person among roughly seven billion carries just under 33 bits.

The drop between 2016 and 2018 is mostly a different population and different attributes (plugins, one of the strongest early signals, were being retired), not a fix. Newer signals such as canvas carry a lot of entropy.

5 · Stable enough to follow you

A good fingerprint needs two things that pull against each other: uniqueness (few people share it) and stability (it stays the same between visits). A signal that is unique but jumpy causes false negatives, the same person looking new; a stable but common one causes false positives, two people merged. Real systems weigh the signals and tolerate small changes.

They have to. In Panopticlick's data, 37.4% of returning visitors showed at least one change within a day or more of their first visit: a browser update, a new font, a zoom level. Trackers do not need an exact match. They need a close one, plus whatever else they have, such as the IP address and visit times. Commercial products say outright that they combine browser signals with server-side data.

That is also why the usual advice fails. Cookies, incognito mode and a VPN all change something the fingerprint does not depend on.

6 · Defences: three strategies

Use the Protection buttons under the figure to see each one.

Reduce and standardise
The W3C's guidance for people writing web standards: add no surface that is not needed, narrow who can use it, round or clamp values, and avoid persistent state. It scores a surface on entropy, detectability, persistence, availability and scope. Done at the standard it protects everyone, but only for new features.
Uniformity (Tor, Mullvad)
Make everyone look the same, so there is nothing to tell people apart. Tor Browser starts with a window size rounded to multiples of 200×100 px, and letterboxing keeps a maximised window in a handful of buckets. It blocks canvas readback, removes plugins and ships a fixed font set. The cost: the fingerprint is itself recognisable as Tor, and a user who resizes the window or has unusual settings can stand out again.
Randomisation, "farbling" (Brave)
Add small noise to what the page reads. Brave makes a random token at start-up, never exposed to sites, and combines it by HMAC with each top-level site. A site therefore sees consistent values within a session, different sites see different values, and the same site sees new values next session. Canvas readback, audio buffers and analyser data, WebGL reads and renderer strings, navigator.plugins and device enumeration are farbled. The page that prompted this figure shows Brave randomising the core count too. In the figure, the farbled traits still narrow the crowd within a session, but those bits stop carrying over, so visits cannot be linked by them.

Why not simply block the APIs? Brave's own account is that blocking helps only at the margins and often breaks sites. And privacy extensions can make you more distinctive, since few people run them and a tracker can notice the tampering and lean on the signals that were left alone.
